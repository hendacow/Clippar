5. Battery safety

Please read this before using the charger

The portable charger contains a rechargeable lithium-ion battery. Lithium-ion batteries store a lot of energy in a small space. If they're damaged, overheated, short-circuited or charged with the wrong equipment, they can vent, catch fire, or explode. This is rare, and it's also the reason these instructions exist.

  • Don't leave it in a hot car. A closed car in an Australian summer gets far hotter than the battery is rated for. This is the single most likely way to damage it.
  • Don't charge it unattended, overnight, or on a bed, sofa or carpet. Charge it on a hard, non-flammable surface.
  • Use the cable and charger supplied, or a reputable equivalent that matches the stated voltage and current.
  • Don't use it if it's damaged — dented, punctured, swollen, leaking, discoloured, or if it smells odd or gets unusually hot. Stop using it immediately and contact us.
  • Don't drop it, crush it, drill it, immerse it in water, or take it apart.
  • Don't short the terminals or carry it loose with keys and coins.
  • Keep it away from children.
  • Charge and store it between [DECISION NEEDED: temperature range from the manufacturer's datasheet].
  • Don't put it in household rubbish or kerbside recycling. Lithium batteries cause fires in waste trucks and recycling facilities. Take it to a battery recycling drop-off — B-cycle lists collection points across Australia.
  • Air travel: power banks must go in your carry-on, never in checked baggage. Check your airline's rules before you fly.

If the battery overheats, swells or catches fire, get away from it and call 000. Don't try to smother a lithium fire with a blanket. Then tell us what happened — we have a legal obligation to report serious incidents, and we need to know.

If this product causes injury or property damage, contact us immediately at clippargolf@gmail.com. Australian law requires us to report a death or serious injury associated with a consumer product to the regulator within two days of becoming aware of it. Reporting it is not us admitting fault — it's how the system is meant to work, and it's how other people get warned.
